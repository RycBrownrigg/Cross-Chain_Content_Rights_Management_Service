\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{chap:conclusion}

This closing chapter restates the contributions, summarizes the key findings, identifies the most important threads for future work, and offers a brief closing reflection.

\section{Summary of Contributions}\label{sec:summary-of-contributions}

Section~\ref{sec:contributions} stated three contributions. We expand each here.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A feasibility result for a cross-chain unified rights record.} Subscription, pay-per-view, and ownership are native modes of a single content record: three storage maps, one priority-ordered check, and shared royalty logic. Single-chain standards (ERC-5643 \citep{cygaar2022}, ERC-4907 \citep{anders2022}, Unlock Protocol \citep{unlockProtocol2026}) express similar access; the contribution makes the record acquirable via XCM and verifiable by storage proof against a trusted CCRMS state root, which the prototype does not yet supply. Issuance was demonstrated on local HRMP; owner-signed cross-chain transfer was demonstrated in the xcm-simulator only. This addresses G7.

\item \textbf{Quantified decentralization--latency and revenue trade-offs.} Prior work endorsed decentralization qualitatively or measured only centralized performance. This dissertation quantifies the trade-offs directly. The latency overhead is $67{,}000\times$ and the throughput penalty is at most $270\times$ against a centralized Express.js baseline (the CCRMS figure is harness-limited); the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, counted per validator, is approximately 600-fold lower than for centralized DRM (Polkadot HHI $\sim$16.7, based on approximately 600 active validators as of September~2026, vs.\ centralized DRM at 10,000). The Monte Carlo simulation shows CCRMS yields a 4.4\% higher mean revenue than the centralized baseline overall (Table~\ref{tab:monte-carlo}), with the advantage concentrated among creators with 10,000 or more followers; the median simulated creator, however, earns less under CCRMS, and the advantage disappears under modest changes to the fee or discovery assumptions. We found no earlier study of blockchain content-rights systems that reports either.

\item \textbf{A cross-chain rights artifact demonstrating XCM sufficiency.} The CCRMS parachain --- \texttt{pallet-content-rights}, \texttt{pallet-rights-verifier}, an ink!\ 6 wrapper contract, an EVM read-only precompile, and benchmark and simulation scripts --- demonstrates that standard XCM instructions with payer--beneficiary decoupling are sufficient for cross-chain rights issuance without custom protocol extensions (all five \texttt{xcm\_*} operations in the xcm-simulator, with ownership transfer limited to the owner's own derived account). Seven implementation specifications (pallet design, XCM message flows, security threat model, royalty algorithm, precompile interface, NFT nesting model, and Snowbridge integration --- the latter covering the integration and its debugging findings, not end-to-end Ethereum-to-CCRMS compatibility, which remains untested) provide design knowledge intended to support future replication and extension. The artifact and specifications are released open source under the MIT license.

\end{enumerate}

The dissertation also documents a DSR cycle applied to a multi-component blockchain prototype, which may serve as a reference for similar projects.

\section{Principal Findings}\label{sec:principal-findings}

Six principal findings emerge from the work:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The primary research question is answered in the affirmative for Polkadot parachains:} a Polkadot parachain with standard XCM instructions and FRAME can deliver the unified rights record. Issuance and access work; the Finding~B fix limits cross-chain ownership transfer to the owner's derived account (xcm-simulator only), and the fifty-holder bound limits the audience size. Of the question's three measures, finality (about 68~s to a finalized cross-chain delivery) and revenue retention were quantified; cost efficiency was not measured. Eight of eleven KPIs in Table~\ref{tab:kpi-results} are met outright; KPIs~2 and~6 at the median and KPI~9 only as an author-audit interpretation (Section~\ref{sec:chapter-7-summary}).
\item \textbf{The major architectural decisions aged well.} The pallet-first decision was well-founded in light of the January 2026 ink! discontinuation; the RMRK 2.0 to \texttt{pallet-nfts} pivot, the unified rights record model, and the cross-chain operation pattern are also vindicated. In retrospect, we would not change these decisions.
\item \textbf{The economic advantage depends on audience size.} CCRMS usually benefits creators with 10,000+ followers but usually loses to centralized platforms below $\sim$6,000 followers, where the assumed algorithmic discovery outweighs fee savings. The mean advantage is small (4.4\%) and depends on unsourced discovery and churn assumptions. While CCRMS earns slightly more on average, the conditional framing better reflects individual creator decisions. Because this advantage lies in audience sizes the current prototype cannot yet serve (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), lifting the holder bound is a prerequisite for it to apply in practice. Therefore the Monte Carlo result characterizes the intended design; it is not an empirical property of the bounded prototype.
\item \textbf{Cross-chain operations are operationally feasible, but latency-bound.} Standard XCM instructions are sufficient for issuance-style rights operations on local HRMP. The destination-chain inclusion delay was measured on a local single-collator testnet with asynchronous backing active and one core per parachain; it describes that environment, not a fundamental limit. Ethereum interoperability is a configured runtime path plus a local ETH~$\to$~AssetHub transfer; the rights pallet was not on that path.
\item \textbf{Standards reimplementation is sometimes preferable to standards adoption} when the reference implementation is unmaintained; the RMRK 2.0 pivot exemplifies this.
\item \textbf{Forward compatibility is a defensive engineering goal.} Configuring the runtime to accept Ethereum-originated XCM messages at minimal additional cost in Phase~4 completed the Snowbridge integration configuration; the gindex and \texttt{forkVersions} debugging findings (Appendix~\ref{app:challenges}) are a useful engineering result, though the Ethereum-to-CCRMS path itself remains untested.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Closing Reflection}\label{sec:closing-reflection}

CCRMS started with the idea that creators deserve more value, and blockchain offers a way to achieve this. We built a prototype, tested its operations, and simulated its economic effects. The premise held only in part: the Monte Carlo simulation shows a small advantage for creators who have outgrown centralized platforms' discovery value, not a universal one, and that advantage rests on assumptions we could not validate. An honest, conditional claim is more valuable than a universal one.

The Polkadot ecosystem evolved significantly during implementation, with major updates like Polkadot~2.0, Snowbridge V2, and others. None invalidated our work because we chose conservatively --- favoring stable primitives, minimizing reliance on non-essential parts, and preferring proven deployment patterns. This highlights a study in \textbf{defensive engineering} and \textbf{conservative, well-documented architecture} as the most durable approach.

CCRMS is a prototype in a single blockchain ecosystem, addressing a specific industry's structural issues. It isn't the future of content rights management, nor do we claim it to be. However, it shows that content rights management can be built on open, decentralized, cryptographically verifiable infrastructure, serving as a starting point for others to build upon.
