\chapter{Results}\label{chap:results}

Section~\ref{sec:technical-results} covers technical results, Section~\ref{sec:economic-results} covers economic results, and Section~\ref{sec:security-user-results} covers security and user-centric results. \section{Technical Results}\label{sec:technical-results}

The technical results address SQ1 (Section~\ref{subsec:latency}), SQ2 (Section~\ref{subsec:comparative-analysis}), and SQ3 (Sections~\ref{subsec:throughput}--\ref{subsec:storage}).

\subsection{Throughput}\label{subsec:throughput}

Table~\ref{tab:throughput} reports 100 concurrent extrinsics per type against a single collator (6-second blocks). All four non-minting extrinsics achieved 100/100 inclusion in one block ($\geq$100 extrinsics per block, $\approx$16.6~TPS). No failure arose from block weight, space, or transaction-pool capacity.

All failures were dispatch errors from the fifty-child bound then in place (removed in \texttt{2865ed0}; Section~\ref{subsec:nft-tokenization}): \texttt{MaxChildrenReached} directly, or cascading \texttt{SubscriptionNotFound}/\texttt{ViewPackNotFound} for dependent extrinsics. The benchmark characterizes storage-design capacity, not parachain throughput. Sustained throughput is in Section~\ref{subsec:stress-test}.

Per-extrinsic TPS is not reported: elapsed time reflects how many blocks the harness waited before observing inclusion (1--4 blocks), not execution cost. Relative costs are in the FRAME weights (Appendix~\ref{app:code}; hand-estimated, not benchmarked).

\subsection{Sustained Throughput (Stress Test)}\label{subsec:stress-test}

Under sustained load (200 accounts, 180~s), the harness recorded 4,713 included \texttt{register\_content} extrinsics in 27 blocks: 26.2~TPS, or 174.6 per block. This is the rate the benchmark client offered, not the chain's capacity. The client is closed-loop: it submits one transaction per account (200 in total), waits until all are included, and only then submits the next batch, so it cannot offer more than about 200 transactions per block. The chain was not saturated: 300 of the same extrinsics used 12.9\% of declared \texttt{ref\_time}, 2.2\% of measured proof size and about 62~ms of a 6~s block (Section~\ref{subsec:block-weight}). The figure is therefore a lower bound on single-collator capacity, which we did not measure. The harness counters are also approximate: at several sampling points, recorded successes plus failures exceed submissions, so some transactions were counted more than once. From about 142~s the client exhausted nonces and issued $\sim$17,000 failed submissions in six seconds, a client limit rather than chain capacity. Only \texttt{register\_content} was measured, and the run predates the fixes for Findings~B, E, I, J and K (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).

\subsection{Latency}\label{subsec:latency}

Single-chain inclusion latency, measured from submission to inclusion in a block, was $\sim$6,000~ms (one parachain block) for 42 of the 53 successful batches in the throughput benchmark; within each batch, all transactions were included in the same block (spread $<$15~ms). Nine batches took longer: seven $\sim$18~s, one $\sim$24~s, and one $\sim$12~s. Two \texttt{subscribe} batches took $\sim$264~s and $\sim$606~s (about 44 and 101 block intervals). The $\sim$264~s batch is the 100-call \texttt{subscribe} batch in which 50 calls failed with \texttt{MaxChildrenReached} (Table~\ref{tab:throughput}). For the $\sim$606~s batch, the node logs needed to determine whether block production paused or whether the transactions waited in the pool were not retained, so it is reported as an unexplained outlier. The median batch latency was $\sim$6.0~s.

In the XCM benchmark, Para~B sent each message with \texttt{sudo(polkadotXcm.send)}, so every message originated from Para~B itself and CCRMS charged execution fees to Para~B's sovereign account. Para~B runs the CCRMS binary, so an ordinary user there can also send a signed \texttt{polkadotXcm.send}; CCRMS then charges fees to, and dispatches under, that user's \texttt{HashedDescription}-derived account (Appendix~\ref{sec:d-finding-b}). The latency benchmark did not exercise this path.

The reliability test (\texttt{scripts/perf/reliability-test.mjs}, commit \texttt{d88ec4e}) killed the collator with SIGKILL, which gives the node no chance to shut down cleanly, restarted it with the same command line, and repeated this three times; one further run used SIGTERM for comparison. Each run monitored the chain for 120~s before the kill and for 120~s after recovery. Table~\ref{tab:reliability} reports medians and ranges.

After a SIGKILL, the node answered RPC requests within 3.8~s and authored a block within 6~s, but that block and the next one or two were later discarded: the first block to become part of the canonical chain was authored 17.9~s after the kill in all three runs, and the first post-kill block was finalized after about 47~s. After a SIGTERM, the first block authored (5.9~s) was kept, and finality followed at 40~s. Both are within the 60~s target (KPI~7). In every run, the last block finalized before the kill remained canonical, the test content and subscription records were byte-for-byte unchanged, and a new transaction was included after restart (KPI~8).

Net chain growth was 18 of 20 expected blocks (90\%) before and after the kill in every run except one (17 of 20 after the kill in the second SIGKILL run). The median interval between consecutive blocks was 6.0~s; the shortfall comes from blocks that the collator authored and then discarded and re-authored, which happened throughout the test, not only after the kill (Finding~F). The 90\% figure therefore measures net chain growth on this single-collator testnet, not time during which the collator was unavailable.

Two practical points follow. Blocks that are not yet finalized can be discarded after a hard failure, so an application should treat a purchase as settled only at finality, about 35--40~s after a block is authored in this environment, not at inclusion. And these figures come from a single collator on one machine; with several collators, block production could continue while one restarts, but this was not tested.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{subsec:sensitivity}

A sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:monte-carlo}) finds the advantage insensitive to content price (expected by construction: price multiplies every platform's gross revenue equally), moderately sensitive to adoption and conversion rates, and strongly sensitive to the audience distribution, the subscription period, the CCRMS fee, and the centralized discovery bonus. Each of the following eliminates the advantage on its own: a median audience of 500 followers, a 24-month subscription period, a CCRMS fee above about 7\%, or a discovery bonus about 25\% larger than assumed.

\subsection{Tested versus Simulated Audience Sizes}\label{subsec:prototype-bound}

The win-rate results in Tables~\ref{tab:audience-tiers} and~\ref{tab:breakeven} characterize the CCRMS design. They do not characterize the artifact as benchmarked.

The prototype originally capped each content item at fifty holders; commit \texttt{2865ed0} removed the cap (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, item~4). CCRMS usually wins only in the Huge and Mega tiers (Table~\ref{tab:audience-tiers}), which imply far more holders per item than any test exercised (at most 60). The prototype can now represent these audiences, but its behavior at that scale is untested.

What the current artifact supports does not depend on audience size: the three rights maps and the priority \texttt{check\_access} (Claim~1, Section~\ref{subsec:claims}); the fee assumptions used in the simulation; and the issuance-style XCM on local HRMP (Claim~2). The economic figures remain a design-level result under Appendix~\ref{app:monte-carlo} (Claim~3, Section~\ref{subsec:claims}).

\subsection{Test Coverage}\label{subsec:test-coverage}

Our unit test suite covers \textbf{18 of the 20 distinct error variants} defined in \texttt{pallet-\allowbreak content-\allowbreak rights} (90 percent), exceeding the Phase~2 objective of 80 percent. One of these, \texttt{NoViewsRemaining}, is no longer reachable through the public API after the Finding~I fix; it is tested by writing an empty view pack directly to storage. Two variants remain untested: \texttt{ItemIdOverflow} requires exhausting a \texttt{u32} counter under normal extrinsic dispatch, and \texttt{NftOperationFailed} is triggered only by internal \texttt{pallet-nfts} state corruption that the mock runtime does not replicate. The comprehensive error variant table is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:security}.

Polkadot's HHI of $\sim$16.7 (as of September 2026, $\sim$600 validators) is well below the U.S.\ DOJ ``unconcentrated'' threshold of 1,500 and orders of magnitude lower than a centralized monopoly at 10,000. The Nakamoto coefficient ($\sim$160--200 for Polkadot vs.\ $\sim$5--7 for Ethereum PoS and 1 for centralized DRM) points the same way: counted per validator, Polkadot's HHI is about 600-fold lower than that of a single-entity centralized system, and nominally 70--120-fold lower than Ethereum PoS and 12--30-fold lower than Solana.

The HHI in Table~\ref{tab:hhi} counts validators, not the entities that control them. Several operators run many Polkadot validators, so the entity-level HHI is higher than 16.7 and the Nakamoto coefficient lower; we did not compute them. The Ethereum row, by contrast, reflects concentration among staking providers, so the rows are not strictly comparable. The conclusion is nonetheless robust: HHI for $N$ equal-sized entities is $10{,}000/N$, so Polkadot's validators would have to be controlled by fewer than seven equal entities to reach the 1,500 threshold.

Table~\ref{tab:hhi} should be read as a statement about safety under a production parachain, not about the test network. The relay-chain HHI indicates who secures CCRMS state once a block is included. The evaluated prototype's liveness is limited to a single collator (Nakamoto coefficient 1 for block production). A deployed CCRMS would inherit Polkadot's validator set for safety and would still need a collator set (or equivalent coretime) for liveness. The validator-level 600-fold HHI contrast with centralized DRM is therefore a property of the intended hosting environment, not of the Zombienet used in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.
