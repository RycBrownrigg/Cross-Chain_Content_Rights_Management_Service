\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{chap:background}

This chapter surveys four themes: blockchain-based content management (Section~\ref{sec:blockchain-content-management}), cross-chain interoperability (Section~\ref{sec:cross-chain-interop}), decentralized monetization models (Section~\ref{sec:decentralized-monetization}), and the challenges and gaps that motivate the research (Section~\ref{sec:challenges-and-gaps}).

\section{Blockchain-Based Content Management}\label{sec:blockchain-content-management}

\subsection{Market Context}\label{subsec:market-context}

Three independent market research firms \citep{marketsandMarkets2025, mordorIntelligence2026, grandViewResearch2025} converge on estimates that the global DRM market is valued at approximately USD~6.7--6.9~billion in 2025--2026, with projections reaching USD~11--14~billion by 2030--2033, representing a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 10--11\%. This growth is primarily driven by streaming services, AI-enabled piracy, and regulatory pressures. Commercial DRM (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady) focuses on content protection rather than monetization, as does most academic work (Section~\ref{subsec:foundational-drm}).

\subsection{Foundational Work on Blockchain-Based DRM}\label{subsec:foundational-drm}

Blockchain-based Digital Rights Management (DRM) is an active research domain. \citet{garbaEtAl2021} incorporate digital watermarking into blockchain technology as a tamper-evident layer; \citet{patilEtAl2022} build on this by introducing the DRMChain hybrid encryption architecture; and \citet{yiEtAl2023} further extend the protection model with a redactable blockchain scheme that combines DRM with perceptual hashing, enabling selective content modification without invalidating ownership proofs. These studies prioritize protection mechanisms over the economics of content creators.

In the music industry, \citet{cirelloEtAl2023} derive a set of design principles for blockchain-based DRM systems through expert interviews and prototype testing, identifying transparency of rights ownership, automated royalty distribution, and programmable licensing as the three foundational requirements. \citet{cherdakovEtAl2023} and \citet{mendozaTelloEtAl2024} propose Web3 implementations that embed these principles in music-streaming smart contracts, demonstrating the feasibility of on-chain licensing but stopping short of cross-chain portability.

The most comprehensive recent work is \citet{madapatiPradhan2025}, whose Ethereum + IPFS framework achieves 409~TPS for registration; this figure is not directly comparable with ours, because their pipeline hashes off-chain. \citet{wangEtAl2025} present a BLS threshold signature scheme achieving 40\,ms aggregation. Two copyright studies \citep{xieLiu2022, xieTang2024a} propose multi-chain frameworks; the later one, a relay-chain design with separate chains for registration, supervision and arbitration, names no implementation platform and is evaluated only qualitatively.

This literature establishes \textbf{blockchain-based DRM as a legitimate research domain}; its consistent limitation is that it emphasizes protection over monetization, a priority CCRMS reverses.

\subsection{NFTs and Intellectual Property Rights}\label{subsec:nfts-ip-rights}

Work on NFTs and intellectual property encompasses legal analysis \citep{uspto2024, davik2025, murray2022, eurojust2024} and standardization proposals \citep{reggianini2025, gattoEtAl2021, dreyerEtAl2021}. A preprint by \citet{darshanEtAl2025} reports that most mainstream NFTs convey no copyright or usage rights to buyers, that only 12--30\% of listings on three major marketplaces include any license information, and that 50--60\% of secondary sales on platforms that do not enforce royalties bypass them; the paper does not describe how these figures were obtained, so we treat them as indicative. A bibliometric and systematic review of 190 NFT articles \citep{lahiriEtAl2026} lists ownership ambiguity and the enforceability of rights among the field's legal challenges, while noting that legal research has so far concentrated on copyright and intellectual property. \citet{cabotNadalEtAl2022} propose an ERC-721 extension enabling recipients to reject unwanted transfers, relevant to our permanent-ownership mode.

The most significant industry development is the February 2025 launch of \textbf{Story Protocol}, the first dedicated intellectual-property Layer-1 blockchain \citep{storyFoundation2025}. Built on the Cosmos SDK with EVM compatibility, it enables programmable IP registration, automated licensing, and on-chain royalty distribution. It is the most direct industry comparator to CCRMS: a single-chain L1 versus a cross-chain parachain.

\section{Cross-Chain Interoperability}\label{sec:cross-chain-interop}

\subsection{Polkadot Architecture}\label{subsec:polkadot-architecture}

Polkadot \citep{wood2016, burdgesEtAl2020} is a heterogeneous multi-chain framework in which parachains share security through a central relay chain and communicate via XCM. The core primitives are parachains, the relay chain, validators, collators, and XCM. \citet{habermeierEtAl2021} and \citet{wood2022} provide the primary technical documentation for the XCM message format and multi-hop features that underpin our cross-chain implementation. The XCM protocol itself has received dedicated academic attention: \citet{morhacEtAl2023a} build ParaSpell, a developer SDK that abstracts XCM message construction across parachains, and \citet{morhacEtAl2023b} demonstrate XCMP interoperability at the protocol level. Together with \citet{morhacEtAl2025}, which proposes the Unispell universal adapter, they form the most coherent body of peer-reviewed academic work on XCM in practice.

Bridge-based systems such as Wormhole and LayerZero accept a message when parties outside both chains' consensus attest to it, so compromising those parties allows forged ownership proofs or redirected royalty payments. Polkadot removes that external layer \citep{burdgesEtAl2020}: the same relay-chain validator set checks every parachain block, and HRMP carries messages through relay-chain storage, so a delivered message is as final as the relay chain itself. Shared security guarantees that each parachain followed its own rules; it does not guarantee that those rules are benign. A sibling parachain can still send well-formed but hostile XCM, so the receiving chain must decide what each origin may do. CCRMS makes that decision through its XCM barrier, origin conversion and pallet-level checks (Appendix~\ref{sec:d-finding-b}).

\subsection{Cross-Chain Technology Surveys}\label{subsec:cross-chain-surveys}

The cross-chain literature matured rapidly from 2022 to 2025, and its surveys classify the same mechanisms in different ways. \citet{renEtAl2023} sort solutions into five categories: sidechains, notary schemes, hashed time lock contracts, relays, and blockchain-agnostic protocols. \citet{maoEtAl2022} propose five overlapping methods (hash locking, notary schemes, distributed private-key control, sidechains and relays, and tight coupling); \citet{dengEtAl2025}, a preprint drawing on more than 150 sources, group mechanisms instead by where verification takes place; and \citet{liEtAl2025a} distinguish \textbf{asset interoperability} (token transfers) from \textbf{data interoperability} (data exchange and contract calls across chains). \citet{bigiottiEtAl2025} narrow the focus to blockchain-agnostic protocols that carry generic messages, and are the only one of these surveys to examine XCM in depth. Three points recur. First, asset transfer is far better developed than message or data exchange; \citet{maoEtAl2022} observe that most projects still lack practical work on data communication. Second, every mechanism retains some trust assumption, and \citet{dengEtAl2025} note that cross-chain exchange cannot be made entirely free of trusted parties. Third, shared-security designs such as Polkadot are described but not evaluated: \citet{dengEtAl2025} list relay-chain shared security as Polkadot's security mechanism without analyzing what a receiving chain must still check. None of the surveys considers rights, licensing, or content metadata crossing chains. CCRMS falls under data interoperability: it moves value through XCM payments and verifies state in part through \texttt{pallet-rights-verifier} Merkle proofs, which require a trusted CCRMS state root that the prototype does not yet supply.
\subsection{Bridges and Snowbridge}\label{subsec:bridges-snowbridge}

Bridge designs differ chiefly in whom the receiving chain must trust. Committee-based bridges replace a single notary with a threshold of signers: Bool Network \citep{yinEtAl2022} uses hidden, rotating committees that hold threshold keys inside trusted execution environments, and the Twin-Token Protocol of \citet{kumarVEtAl2025} relies on a threshold-signing Internet Computer subnet to mirror token transfers between EVM chains; both remain safe only while an honest threshold of signers (and, for Bool, the trusted hardware) holds. \citet{chenKEtAl2023} add reputation scoring to a relay-chain architecture, which filters misbehaving nodes but does not prove what happened on the source chain. MAP \citep{caoEtAl2025} verifies each source chain with on-chain light clients and zero-knowledge proofs, yet still assumes an honest two-thirds of its relay chain's stake and, by the authors' own analysis, is only as secure as the weakest connected chain. \citet{maricEtAl2025} prove in Isabelle/HOL that a lock-and-mint bridge is fail-safe, meaning users can always recover their assets, under the explicit assumption that the validators who post state roots are honest. Two observations follow for CCRMS. Each design reduces trust rather than removing it, and each concerns token transfer rather than authorizing arbitrary calls on the receiving chain. That second question, which origin may invoke which operation, remains even under shared security (Section~\ref{subsec:polkadot-architecture}).

The most operationally significant development in blockchain infrastructure is \textbf{Snowbridge}, a trust-minimized bridge that enables interoperability between Polkadot and Ethereum. Snowbridge V2 was officially launched on the Polkadot mainnet on November 7, 2025, and includes support for arbitrary Ethereum contract calls from Polkadot, a 50\% fee reduction, and unordered message execution \citep{snowfork2025a}. The associated Polkassembly proposal \citep{snowfork2025b} documents more than a year of operational stability with no on-chain downtime. We developed and tested our integration against V1 and have not tested it against V2.

\subsection{Polkadot 2.0 and JAM}\label{subsec:polkadot-2-jam}

Two ecosystem developments shaped our implementation. \textbf{Polkadot 2.0} groups four changes that reached the production network during 2025 \citep{parityUpgrade2025, polkadotNetwork2025}. Asynchronous backing lets collators build on parachain blocks that the relay chain has not yet included, which halves the parachain block time from 12 to 6 seconds and raises the execution time available per block from 0.5 to 2 seconds \citep{polkadotWikiAsyncBacking}. Agile Coretime replaces fixed-term slot auctions with the sale of core time, in bulk periods on a dedicated Coretime chain or block by block on demand \citep{woodRFC1}. Elastic scaling lets one parachain use several cores at once \citep{paritySdkElasticScaling}. XCM~v5 adds, among other changes, a \texttt{PayFees} instruction that succeeds \texttt{BuyExecution} \citep{aguirreRFC105}, an \texttt{InitiateTransfer} instruction that combines several asset-transfer types in one message \citep{catangiuRFC100}, and origin aliasing through \texttt{AliasOrigin}. Each bears on CCRMS: our testnet ran with asynchronous backing, elastic scaling is the main remaining route to higher throughput for a single chain (Section~\ref{subsec:26-tps-context}), and \texttt{AliasOrigin} would let remote users act as their local accounts (Appendix~\ref{sec:e-delegation}). \textbf{JAM} (Join-Accumulate Machine, \citet{wood2024}) is expected to succeed the relay-chain model within the Polkadot ecosystem, with a public testnet since January 2026; as of September~2026 no mainnet date has been announced.

\subsection{Cross-Chain Copyright Management as a Research Target}\label{subsec:cross-chain-copyright-research}

Work on blockchain copyright management spans legal analysis \citep{buzu2021, mareckova2024}, qualitative study of rights holders \citep{oreroEtAl2023}, and conceptual multi-chain designs \citep{xieLiu2022, xieTang2024a}; little of it involves cross-chain execution. The one deployed multi-chain system we found, Eluvio's Content Fabric \citep{munsonEtAl2022}, gates access to content on token ownership recorded on another chain, but by its own description it learns that ownership from a trusted oracle that signs the result, not through messages secured by a shared validator set. We found no work that combines subscription, pay-per-view, and ownership in a single record that users on other chains can acquire through such messages. This gap, together with the architectural analysis above, motivates selecting XCM and FRAME as the implementation substrate and Polkadot over single-chain or bridge-based alternatives.

XCM is preferred over Wormhole and LayerZero because it is native to the relay chain and adds no trusted party beyond the relay-chain validators and the sending chain's own runtime. Its structured instruction format also encodes rights operations as typed, on-chain-interpretable messages rather than opaque byte payloads, preserving rights metadata across chain boundaries (gap~G3). FRAME is the implementation framework of choice over EVM and CosmWasm alternatives because its composable module system allows all three access modes to share a single pallet and access-check function, its weight benchmarking provides deterministic fee calculations, and its typed storage API exposes rights state as queryable on-chain objects via the RPC layer.

\section{Decentralized Monetization Models}\label{sec:decentralized-monetization}

Creator monetization is a growing commercial target for blockchain systems: one market report values blockchain-based content monetization at USD~499.52~million in 2024 and projects USD~2.74~billion by 2030 \citep{threeSixtyIResearch2025}. The work reviewed here falls into three strands: subscription and purchase models, pay-per-view and the micropayment problem, and the smart-contract layer that implements them.

\subsection{Subscription, Pay-Per-View, and Purchase Models}\label{subsec:subscription-ppv-purchase}

Earlier work on blockchain subscription and pay-per-view models includes \citet{munson2019, steem2025, banerjeeEtAl2020}. Most of these works examine blockchain incentives without addressing the micropayment problem. Two recent works more closely align with our aims. \citet{osemwegie2025} reviews blockchain-based media monetization, including micropayments, tokens, and NFTs, and identifies the problems CCRMS targets but does not propose a system. \citet{shah2025} outlines an Ethereum smart-contract framework in which oracles report usage from streaming platforms and a registry contract splits royalties among rights holders; it is the nearest precedent for our royalty splitting, but it is unimplemented, runs on a single chain, and does not propagate royalties through derivative works. Other work on royalty automation is scarce; \citet{reggianini2025} addresses part of the licensing standardization problem for European NFTs. CCRMS implements a comparable royalty split in a FRAME pallet, in which all split payments within one extrinsic succeed or fail together (Appendix~\ref{sec:d-finding-k}). \citet{wamugo2024} provides empirical data on NFT adoption by media organizations in Kenya, demonstrating that blockchain monetization is under active consideration in emerging markets.

The EVM ecosystem has standardized parts of this design space. ERC-5643 \citep{cygaar2022} extends ERC-721 with subscription expiry and renewal; ERC-4907 \citep{anders2022} separates an NFT's owner from a time-limited user; and Unlock Protocol \citep{unlockProtocol2026} deploys renewable, optionally transferable membership keys in production. Superfluid \citep{superfluid2026} addresses recurring payments through continuous token streams. Together, they show that time-bound and ownership-based access can be expressed as token state on a single chain. What they do not provide is (i) counter-based pay-per-view within the same record, or (ii) a way for a user on another chain to acquire or prove these rights without a bridge. CCRMS targets that intersection. On Polkadot, Unique Network \citep{uniqueNetwork2026} provides native NFT nesting at the runtime level, an alternative to the \texttt{pallet-nfts} reimplementation discussed in Appendix~\ref{sec:rmrk-to-pallet-nfts}.

\subsection{Pay-Per-View and the Micropayment Problem}\label{subsec:ppv-micropayment}

Pay-per-view models have historically struggled on blockchain platforms because gas fees have often exceeded content costs. \citet{goyalEtAl2019} introduce Gringotts, a secure micropayment system, but do not address the core fee issue. \citet{chegenizadehEtAl2024} directly address the micropayment verification layer, proposing a multi-agent, privacy-preserving approach for resource-constrained offline devices that reduces per-transaction overhead through smart-contract batching; their work supports our strategic decision to model PPV as a counter rather than a sequence of individual on-chain transactions.

\subsection{Smart Contracts as the Implementation Layer}\label{subsec:smart-contracts-impl}

Surveys of the smart contract literature \citep{ante2020, linEtAl2022, singhEtAl2024a, quanEtAl2024} establish the field as mature; Parity's ink! blog posts \citep{parityTechnologiesInk3_2022, parityTechnologiesInk_2022} were the primary technical references during implementation. The most consequential development is the \textbf{discontinuation of ink! in January 2026} \citep{inkAlliance2026}, discussed as gap G8 and in Appendix~\ref{sec:ink-discontinuation}.

The prototype addresses G1 and G7 directly, and G4 and G5 within its scope (a counter-based pay-per-view model and a local-testnet benchmark). Its contributions to G2 and G3 are partial: renewal is automated on CCRMS only, and metadata verification requires a trusted CCRMS state root. G6 and G8 depend on factors outside the prototype's control. The collective contribution is that a single working prototype demonstrates feasibility for a category of systems that the literature has addressed only partially.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:chapter-2-summary}

We surveyed the academic and industry literature on blockchain-based content management, cross-chain interoperability, and decentralized monetization. The foundational primitives are in place; commercial interest in decentralized content monetization is growing; and existing work addresses the problem only in parts, mostly through legal analysis, conceptual designs, or single-chain systems. CCRMS addresses two of the eight identified gaps directly and the others in part (Section~\ref{sec:challenges-and-gaps}) by deploying a unified rights record on a Polkadot parachain.
