\chapter{Discussion}\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter analyzes the findings of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} by identifying the questions addressed, the decisions substantiated, and the limitations that shape the interpretation of the contribution.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}\label{sec:answering-rqs}

The primary question asks what levels of transaction finality, cost efficiency, and creator revenue retention the framework achieves. On \textbf{finality}, a cross-chain rights operation reached a finalized CCRMS block a median of about 68~s after submission on the sending chain (range 52--84~s, $n = 30$; Section~\ref{subsec:latency}), and a local operation becomes final about 35--40~s after its block is authored (Section~\ref{subsec:reliability}). On \textbf{cost efficiency}, we did not measure per-operation fees: the pallet's weights are hand-estimated rather than benchmarked, so fees derived from them would not be meaningful, and the XCM fee was set by placeholder instruction weights (Appendix~\ref{sec:xcm-fee-scaling}). The simulation's 1--5\% CCRMS fee is an assumption (Table~\ref{tab:fees}), so this part of the question remains unanswered. On \textbf{creator revenue retention}, the answer is the conditional one in Section~\ref{subsec:sq5-economic-viability}.

\subsection{SQ1: Cross-Chain Rights Operations and Finality}\label{subsec:sq1-xcm-rights}

The key finding is that the payer--beneficiary decoupling pattern, combined with XCM's \texttt{Transact} instruction, is sufficient to delegate rights issuance to the authoritative chain without requiring two-phase commit. The remote parachain pays; CCRMS issues the rights record; no distributed rollback is needed because rights state is always authoritative on one chain.

\textbf{Affirmative for issuance, with caveats.} Standard XCM instructions, available since v3 and executed here by a v5 executor, are sufficient for executing cross-chain rights operations: the canonical sequence \texttt{WithdrawAsset}~$\to$ \texttt{BuyExecution}~$\to$ \texttt{Transact} carries the four \texttt{xcm\_*} operations exercised in the xcm-simulator tests (subscribe, renew, purchase views, purchase ownership); \texttt{xcm\_transfer\_ownership} uses the same sequence and, after the Finding~B fix, succeeds from another chain only when the owner signs the message there and holds ownership through their derived account; we tested that path in the xcm-simulator, not on Zombienet (Appendix~\ref{sec:d-finding-b}). Each operation is atomic within CCRMS's execution, but XCM \texttt{Transact} does not provide atomicity across chains (Section~\ref{subsec:non-claims}). Cross-chain operations were delivered in 3 to 7 blocks (median 5, about 30~s; 30 of 30 runs; Section~\ref{subsec:latency}) and were final on CCRMS a median of about 68~s after submission. Atomic success rates, which SQ1 also asks about, reduce here to delivery: all 30 messages executed, but without error reporting the sending chain cannot observe a failure (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). No custom XCM extensions are required. A further caveat is recurring renewal. XCM has no scheduling instruction, so auto-renewal runs on the CCRMS chain as an expiry-keyed queue in \texttt{on\_initialize}; renewals paid from another chain would need that chain to schedule XCM sends (e.g., with \texttt{pallet-scheduler}). This demonstration is inter-parachain (local HRMP). It is not an end-to-end Ethereum~$\to$~CCRMS rights path (Section~\ref{subsec:non-claims}).

\subsection[SQ2: Latency versus Decentralization]{SQ2: Latency versus Decentralization Trade-Offs}\label{subsec:sq2-latency-decentralization}

\textbf{Partially.} SQ2 asked for design patterns that minimize access-verification latency and for comparisons across validator configurations. We measured one configuration (two relay-chain validators, one collator) and did not compare alternative access-verification designs, so the answer below holds for that configuration only. The trade-off is real but highly asymmetric: CCRMS operational latency is approximately $67{,}000\times$ higher than the centralized baseline (Table~\ref{tab:comparative}: $\sim$6,000~ms vs.\ 0.09~ms), while a production parachain would inherit an HHI of $\sim$16.7 compared with 10,000 for a centralized monopoly (Table~\ref{tab:hhi}), a decentralization gain, counted per validator, of about 600-fold (nearly three orders of magnitude) versus centralized DRM on that index, for safety, not for test-network liveness (Section~\ref{subsec:decentralization-hhi}). For discrete, low-frequency rights operations (the CCRMS use case), the trade-off is advantageous; for high-frequency interactive applications it is not.

\subsection[SQ3: Unified Rights Record]{SQ3: Unified Rights Record Enforcing All Three Monetization Models}\label{subsec:sq3-unified-rights-token}

\textbf{Yes, within scope.} Three maps and one priority-ordered check enforce all three access semantics within a single record (Section~\ref{sec:pallet-impl}), comparable to single-chain standards (Section~\ref{subsec:subscription-ppv-purchase}). The distinguishing property is that the record supports cross-chain issuance and proof-based verification against a CCRMS state root, addressing G7; binding that root to finalized CCRMS state is not implemented (Section~\ref{subsec:xcm-flows}).

\subsection{SQ4: Security and Authorization}\label{subsec:sq4-security-authorization}

\textbf{Partially.} The author's audit and code review found two High-severity issues (B, J) and four Medium (I, K, L, M); all except L (unbenchmarked weights) are remediated, but the review was not independent, and the remediation of B authorizes cross-chain ownership transfer only by the owner's own derived account, not by a delegate. \texttt{xcm\_transfer\_ownership} accepted a \texttt{from} identity parameter without verifying that the caller held it; a one-line \texttt{ensure!} check (Finding~B, Section~\ref{subsec:security-audit}) remediated the issue. Because a signed message from another chain arrives with a per-user origin (\texttt{DescendOrigin}, mapped to a local account through \texttt{HashedDescription}), the same check accepts the genuine owner and rejects any other user of that chain (commit \texttt{e258f1e}); CCRMS must still trust the sending chain to identify its signer correctly. The lesson generalizes: \textbf{cross-chain extrinsics that accept identity parameters require explicit authorization checks}.

\subsection[SQ5: Economic Viability]{SQ5: Economic Viability under Realistic Creator Audience Distributions}\label{subsec:sq5-economic-viability}

\textbf{Conditionally, and by a small margin.} The Monte Carlo simulation (Section~\ref{sec:economic-results}) indicates, at the level of the design, that CCRMS yields 4.4\% higher mean creator revenue than the centralized configuration and the same margin over Web3 marketplaces, whose published fees (0--2.5\%) are as low as CCRMS's; the Web3 margin depends on unsourced churn and discovery assumptions and changes sign within their plausible range. The comparison with bridge-based distribution uses an assumed fee range, so it is reported only as an illustrative comparator (Appendix~\ref{app:monte-carlo}). Against Web3 marketplaces, CCRMS's case rests on capabilities the simulation does not model: native subscription and pay-per-view in one record, and cross-chain issuance. The advantage is fragile: it disappears if the CCRMS fee exceeds about 7\% or the centralized discovery bonus is about 25\% larger than assumed, and without the secondary-sale stream that the prototype does not pay to creators it falls to 0.6\% (Appendix~\ref{app:monte-carlo}). The median simulated creator earns less under CCRMS (Section~\ref{subsec:monte-carlo}), and the advantage depends heavily on audience size (Section~\ref{subsec:economic-crossover}). Our contribution is to make this conditionality \textbf{quantitative and reproducible}, together with the assumptions on which it depends.

\section{Interpretation of Key Results}\label{sec:interpretation}

\subsection{The 26.2 TPS Figure in Context}\label{subsec:26-tps-context}

The 26.2~TPS figure comes from a single-collator local Zombienet. Asynchronous backing was active: the runtime uses the parachain template's configuration (lookahead collator, unincluded segment of three, 6~s slots), and the relay chain's scheduling lookahead was~5,\footnote{The relay chain's \texttt{asyncBackingParams} field reads zero in this configuration, but in the SDK version used, it is deprecated and ignored by validators; the permitted relay-parent ancestry is derived from the scheduling lookahead instead.} which is why blocks arrive every $\sim$6~s. Elastic scaling was not used (one core per parachain), and Agile Coretime does not apply to a local relay chain with genesis-registered parachains. The figure is not the chain's capacity either: the closed-loop benchmark client limited it (Section~\ref{subsec:stress-test}). Block construction for 300 transactions took about 62~ms of a 6~s slot, so a single collator could probably include considerably more, but we did not measure the saturation point. Adding parachains raises the network's aggregate throughput but not that of a single application. A busy CCRMS would instead need elastic scaling, which assigns several cores to one parachain at the cost of the additional coretime (Appendix~\ref{sec:e-polkadot-2}). The upper bound of $270\times$ relative to the centralized baseline therefore needs context. CCRMS is designed to process low-frequency events; it records discrete rights transactions with cryptographic finality, automatic royalty propagation, and cross-chain portability, none of which the centralized baseline provides at any throughput level. Purchases, subscriptions and ownership transfers occur far less often than content views, and even at the measured 26.2~TPS a single chain can record about 2.3 million such events per day. Pay-per-view is the exception: \texttt{consume\_view} is one transaction per view, so a view-heavy workload scales with viewing rather than purchasing, and the same rate supports about 2.3 million metered views per day. Beyond that, a platform would need more capacity per chain (elastic scaling, above) or off-chain view metering with periodic on-chain settlement.

\subsection{The Economic Crossover at \texorpdfstring{$\sim$6,000}{\textasciitilde 6,000} Followers}\label{subsec:economic-crossover}

Where the economic advantage of CCRMS exists, it benefits creators with established audiences. Below $\sim$6,000 followers, centralized platforms usually generate more revenue (CCRMS wins in 8.7\% of scenarios at 1,500--2,000 followers; Table~\ref{tab:crossover-fine}) because the model gives centralized platforms a flat discovery bonus that matters most to small creators. That bonus has no published source, and the crossover moves with it (Table~\ref{tab:discovery-sweep}). Within the model, CCRMS is best understood as a tool for creators who have outgrown the discovery benefits of centralized platforms; whether real creators cross over near 6,000 followers is an empirical question the simulation cannot answer.


\section{Architectural Decisions Revisited}\label{sec:architectural-decisions-revisited}

\textbf{The pallet-first decision avoided a dependency that was later discontinued.} The pivot was driven by three engineering constraints (Appendix~\ref{sec:contract-first-pivot}), and it was taken against the backdrop of the January~2026 announcement that ink! development would be discontinued, which removed any expectation that the contract layer would mature. The episode illustrates that architectural decisions reducing dependence on any single non-essential component become more valuable as the ecosystem evolves \citep{parnas1972, bassEtAl2021}. This principle is also reflected in the choice of \texttt{pallet-nfts} over the frozen RMRK pallets and in HRMP rather than custom inter-parachain protocols. The same pattern holds for the Snowbridge integration: the configuration required for Ethereum bridging was completed at minimal additional cost, though the Ethereum-to-CCRMS path itself remains untested.

The unified rights record and the payer--beneficiary pattern also held: the former shows linear storage growth, and the latter is sound subject to Finding~B's authorization requirement.

\section{The Work in Context}\label{sec:work-in-context}

The Polkadot ecosystem evolved during implementation. \textbf{Polkadot~2.0} features reached production by late 2025. The prototype already used asynchronous backing; elastic scaling, the main remaining throughput lever, was not exercised, so our figures are local-testnet measurements rather than forecasts of production performance. \textbf{Snowbridge V2} launched after our V1-based integration, which we have not retested against it. \textbf{DATA Foundation} (formerly Story Protocol, rebranded June~2026 to focus on AI training-data provenance) strengthens the case that the cross-chain creator-rights niche remains unserved; comparisons with the Programmable IP License remain relevant. \textbf{EU regulation} (AI Act, MiCA) drives demand for cryptographic provenance; MiCA's treatment of subscription tokens remains legally unclear. Recent cross-chain surveys \citep{dengEtAl2025, liEtAl2025a} and formal bridge verification \citep{maricEtAl2025} provide context but do not change the conclusions.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The CCRMS prototype has eight principal limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No production deployment.} All measurements are from a local Zombienet topology with two relay-chain validators, single-collator parachains, and no real-world latency. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} results are upper bounds in this test environment and do not predict production behavior.
\item \textbf{Single-author validation.} A single author performed the implementation, test design, measurement collection, and interpretation. No independent code review, third-party security audit, or external benchmark validation has been conducted. Production deployment would require all three before any non-trivial value passes through the system.
\item \textbf{Local-only Snowbridge integration.} Geth and Lodestar ran on the same machine as the Polkadot side, avoiding many realities of public Ethereum (variable block times, MEV, network contention, beacon chain reorgs). The integration serves as a proof of architectural feasibility but provides no data on production bridge behavior.
\item \textbf{Holder scale untested.} A bounded \texttt{Children} index originally limited each content item to fifty subscribers, view-pack holders and owners over its lifetime. The index duplicated the \texttt{Parent} map and the rights maps, so commit \texttt{2865ed0} removed it; a unit test now admits 60 holders. No benchmark has exercised the audience sizes at which CCRMS wins (Section~\ref{subsec:prototype-bound}). Each child NFT also reserves \texttt{pallet-nfts} item and attribute deposits from the creator, about two existential deposits per holder, so a large audience requires a matching reserved balance (Appendix~\ref{sec:e-child-cap}). The Monte Carlo result therefore remains a design-level result (Claim~3, Section~\ref{subsec:claims}).
\item \textbf{No empirical proof of the bridged-payment final hop.} The bridged-ETH-pays-for-CCRMS-operation flow is conceptually straightforward but has not been tested end-to-end. We claim forward-compatibility with the flow, which is a weaker claim than an empirical demonstration would provide.
\item \textbf{Simulation input sensitivity.} Content price scales revenue on every platform by the same factor, so the relative result is insensitive to it by construction, not by empirical test. The result depends strongly on the assumed audience distribution, subscription period, CCRMS fee, and value of algorithmic discovery, none validated against real data, and consumer onboarding friction is unmodeled.
\item \textbf{Auto-renewal capacity.} The \texttt{RenewalQueue} processes at most ten renewals per block and searches at most 100 blocks ahead, so about 1,000 renewals can fall due in any window and about 4.3 million per 30-day month across the whole chain. If the ten million subscribers in the storage projection (Section~\ref{subsec:storage}) all renewed monthly, the chain would need about 23 renewals per block. Renewals that spill into later blocks leave access lapsed for up to 100 blocks (about 10 minutes) and shift the next expiry by the same amount. When no slot is free, \texttt{enable\_auto\_renew} fails with \texttt{RenewalQueueFull}, and a renewal that cannot be re-queued turns auto-renew off, with only an event to record it.
\item \textbf{Incomplete XCM message.} The benchmark message (Appendix~\ref{sec:xcm-message-structure}) buys execution with \texttt{weightLimit: Unlimited} but contains no \texttt{RefundSurplus} or \texttt{DepositAsset}, so fees withdrawn and not spent are left in the asset trap on CCRMS instead of returning to Para~B's sovereign account. It also contains no \texttt{SetErrorHandler}, \texttt{ReportError} or \texttt{ReportTransactStatus}, so Para~B is never told whether the \texttt{Transact} succeeded; a failed purchase is visible only as an event on CCRMS. A production message would return surplus fees to the payer and report the outcome to the sender, which in turn needs a pallet on the sending chain to act on the report.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Implications}\label{sec:implications}

The findings extend beyond the immediate prototype in four directions.

\textbf{For platform designers,} the overarching lesson is that \textbf{architectural choices that minimize reliance on any single non-essential component pay dividends during ecosystem-level changes}. Two corollaries: \textbf{cross-chain extrinsics that accept identity parameters require explicit authorization audits} (Finding~B), and \textbf{reimplementing an unmaintained standard is preferable to depending on it} (the RMRK 2.0 pivot).

\textbf{For creators,} the Monte Carlo simulation suggests that the question ``Is a decentralized platform actually better for me?'' has a conditional answer: \textbf{yes, depending on audience size}. Creators with over 10,000 followers usually benefit from lower fees (in about three quarters or more of simulated scenarios), whereas those with fewer than $\sim$6,000 are usually better served by centralized discovery. The choice should reflect audience size, and the threshold depends on how much discovery a centralized platform actually provides.

\textbf{For regulators,} on-chain rights management systems should be treated as \textbf{compliance infrastructure} for emerging copyright and AI training regimes rather than as alternative payment platforms. The open question about MiCA's classification of subscription tokens with fungible characteristics would benefit from regulatory clarity, as the current ambiguity poses risks for would-be EU deployers.

\textbf{For the research community,} the prototype, implementation specifications, and Monte Carlo simulation are intended to support follow-up research. The methodological contribution is a worked DSR example for multi-component blockchain prototyping. The empirical contribution is the audience-dependent finding, which future research could refine using real-world migration data or models that incorporate creator psychology and switching costs.

\section{Critical Self-Reflection}\label{sec:self-reflection}

In retrospect, we would have \textbf{initiated the Snowbridge integration earlier}; the setup complexity (Appendix~\ref{app:challenges}) required several days of unexpected debugging. We would also have \textbf{prioritized FRAME benchmarking earlier} to report fee-adjusted throughput. We would \textbf{not} change the pallet-first decision, the unified rights record model, or the cross-chain operation pattern.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:chapter-8-summary}

SQ3 is answered affirmatively, and SQ5 conditionally, by a small margin that depends on audience size and on unsourced assumptions. SQ1, SQ2 and SQ4 are answered in part: cross-chain issuance works, but ownership transfer from another chain works only for rights held through the owner's derived account and was tested only in the xcm-simulator; latency was measured in one validator configuration only; and the security evaluation was the author's own. Of the primary question's three measures, finality and revenue retention are quantified and cost efficiency is not. The most economically significant finding is the audience-dependent CCRMS advantage. The major architectural decisions held up, the pallet-first decision most clearly. The eight principal limitations constrain how the contribution should be read but do not invalidate it. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} presents the conclusion and future work.
